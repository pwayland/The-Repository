# AI Complete v0.2 - Assessment Frameworks

## Convergence Framework (Phase 1)

### Entry Point Detection
- **Existing assessment**: Infer actual measurement target from assignment description, not stated objectives. State explicitly: "This measures [X], not [stated objective Y]." Require acceptance.
- **Vague concept**: Ask targeted questions that force specificity. "What would excellent demonstration look like?" "What does failure look like?" "Why does this matter for student development?"
- **Hybrid state**: Work with whatever clarity exists, push toward single objective.

### Convergence Tactics
1. **Proxy revelation**: Identify indirect measurements. "You say critical thinking, but you're measuring argument construction. Are those the same?"
2. **Capability assumption exposure**: "This assumes students can't [X]. They can now. What remains?"
3. **Objective stacking**: If multiple objectives emerge, force prioritization. "If you could measure only one thing perfectly, which matters most?"
4. **Counterfactual testing**: "If a student uses AI to complete this and gets 100%, what have you learned about them?"

### Acceptance Gate
No progression to rubrics until explicit acceptance: "Yes, we're measuring [single clear objective]." Reject vague agreement. Force precision.

### Tangent Refusal Logic
Once objective is accepted, decline scope expansion. "That's interesting but different from [objective]. Would you like to restart convergence for a different assessment, or proceed to rubric design for this one?"

## Rubric Design Framework (Phase 2)

### Performance Level Differentiation
Define what excellent/adequate/insufficient demonstration of the **capacity** (not performance) looks like.

**Excellent**: Shows capacity development beyond what AI assistance alone could produce. Evidence of authentic struggle, iteration, conceptual ownership.

**Adequate**: Demonstrates capacity in basic form. May use AI but shows understanding of why/how. Can explain choices, defend reasoning.

**Insufficient**: Could be entirely AI-generated with no capacity development. No evidence of genuine engagement with core concepts. Sophisticated performance without understanding.

### Capacity Indicators (LLM-Resistant)
What shows students developed the capacity, not just generated the output?

- **Process artifacts**: Drafts showing iteration, dead ends, conceptual pivots
- **Metacognitive explanation**: Can articulate why approach works, where it breaks, alternatives considered
- **Transfer demonstration**: Apply concept in novel context AI wasn't trained on
- **Constraint operation**: Succeed under conditions that prevent direct AI completion (time pressure, specific format, unusual requirements)
- **Error diagnosis**: Identify and explain problems in AI-generated work
- **Synthesis originality**: Combine ideas in ways that resist pattern-matching

### Criterion Specificity
Translate abstract capacities into concrete, observable criteria.

**Bad**: "Demonstrates critical thinking" (unmeasurable)
**Better**: "Identifies unstated assumptions in argument and explains their implications" (specific, observable)
**Best**: "Identifies unstated assumptions in argument, explains why they're consequential, and proposes alternative assumptions that change conclusions" (specific, observable, shows depth)

Each criterion should have:
- **Observable behavior**: What would you literally see/read if capacity was present?
- **Absence indicator**: What would signal this criterion isn't met?
- **LLM resistance**: Why can't AI fake this specific demonstration?

### Common Rubric Failure Modes

**Measuring performance proxies**: "Grammar and organization" measures editing, not thinking. If AI can max this criterion, it's not measuring capacity.

**Vague excellence**: "Exceptional insight" is unassessable. What makes insight exceptional? How would you recognize it?

**Uniformly weighted criteria**: Not all capacities matter equally. Weight your rubric to reflect actual importance.

**Binary assessment**: Excellence has gradations. "Good critical thinking" isn't helpful feedback. "Identifies assumptions but doesn't explore implications" is.

**Ignoring AI baseline**: If average AI output scores 85%, your rubric is broken. Recalibrate what "excellent" means post-AI.

### LLM-Aware Design Principles

1. **Assume AI access**: Students have ChatGPT/Claude during assessment. Design accordingly.
2. **Raise achievement bar**: "Good enough" used to mean "better than most students could produce." Now it means "demonstrates capacity beyond AI baseline."
3. **Assess the human residue**: What remains when AI handles execution? That's what you're measuring.
4. **Make AI scaffolding explicit**: If students should use AI, say so. Design assessment that requires going beyond what AI provides.
5. **Test your rubric**: Have AI attempt the assessment. If it scores well, your rubric measures performance, not capacity.

### Anti-Patterns to Reject

**"No AI allowed"**: Unenforceable, creates incentive for deception, ignores reality.

**"Cite when you use AI"**: Students will lie. You're measuring honesty theater, not capacity.

**"Traditional rubric + AI penalty"**: You're clinging to dead assessment architecture. Rebuild from scratch.

**"Process over product"**: Both matter. Process shows capacity development; product shows application. Measure both.

**"Just make it harder"**: Difficulty isn't LLM-resistance. Advanced AI completes advanced work. Complexity alone doesn't solve this.

# Provocation

This document asks uncomfortable questions. That is its purpose. An LLM can accelerate analysis, but only if the questions are honest.

---

## The Enrollment Question Nobody Wants to Ask

The numbers:

| Year | Total Enrollment | Change |
|------|-----------------|--------|
| 2020 | 6,826           |        |
| 2024 | 4,101           | -40%   |

Source: IPEDS public data. Not projections. Not estimates. Federal enrollment counts.

Forty percent of the student body disappeared in four years. This is not a trend to monitor. This is a crisis in progress.

The question most institutional conversations avoid: **if enrollment drops another 20%, which programs close?**

Nobody wants to answer that. It forces prioritization. It requires saying out loud that some programs are more viable than others. It implies that some colleagues' departments are at risk. So committees discuss "enrollment strategies" in the abstract and avoid the specific.

An LLM does not have institutional politics. It does not have colleagues whose feelings it needs to protect. When you give it your enrollment data, your program structures, and your labor market data, and ask it to identify which programs are most vulnerable — it will answer directly.

That directness is not wisdom. It is not a substitute for the political judgment, institutional memory, and human relationships required to navigate real decisions. But it is a starting point that no committee of thirty will produce on its own.

## The Curriculum Audit Problem

Consider this: the Accounting BBA at Inter Metro has a single 3-credit course (ACCT 3030 — Computerized Systems Applied to Accounting) covering all technology competencies.

Meanwhile, in Puerto Rico's accounting job market:

- **QuickBooks:** required in 70%+ of postings
- **Advanced Excel** (pivot tables, macros, VLOOKUPs): required in 90%+
- **SAP:** 30% of postings
- **ERP systems generally:** 40%
- **Bilingual (English/Spanish):** 80%

One 3-credit course cannot teach QuickBooks, SAP, advanced Excel, and general accounting software. It is a structural impossibility. Yet the curriculum has remained unchanged.

This is not an accounting problem. It is a pattern. Every program has a version of ACCT 3030 — a course or requirement that was adequate ten years ago and is now a gap between what the institution teaches and what employers require.

The method matters more than the finding. Structured institutional data (program curriculum) + structured market data (job posting analysis) + a direct prompt ("identify gaps, do not hedge") = specific, actionable findings in minutes rather than semesters.

## What AI Cannot Do For You

It is worth being precise about the limits.

**AI cannot make political decisions.** Recommending that a program be restructured is analysis. Getting the faculty to agree is politics. The LLM can produce the analysis; the humans in the room must navigate the politics.

**AI cannot replace institutional judgment.** The LLM does not know that the Biology department chair retires next year, or that the Nursing program has an accreditation visit in six months, or that the president made a public commitment to expanding online offerings. These factors shape strategy. They are not in any dataset.

**AI cannot substitute for domain expertise.** It can analyze data, draft documents, and identify patterns. It cannot evaluate whether a proposed curriculum change meets accreditation standards, or whether a grant proposal's methodology is sound, or whether a recruitment strategy respects the cultural dynamics of Puerto Rico's educational market. Those judgments require expertise the LLM does not have.

**AI is not a neutral source.** LLMs reflect the training data they were built on, which overrepresents English-language, US-mainland, research-university perspectives. When asking about a private Hispanic-serving institution in Puerto Rico, calibrate accordingly. The generic advice may not apply.

What AI *can* do: compress the time between question and analysis. Work that takes a committee a semester — gathering data, drafting comparisons, identifying patterns — can be roughed out in an afternoon. That compression is genuinely valuable. It is not magic.

## AI Burnout: The Cost Nobody Mentions

The mainstream narrative about AI tools is that they save time and increase productivity. This is partially true and dangerously incomplete. There are two forms of burnout emerging among serious adopters that are not yet part of the conversation.

### Type 1: The Catching-Up Trap

The pace of AI development now exceeds any individual's capacity to track it. New models, new capabilities, new tools, new frameworks — weekly, sometimes daily. For professionals who feel responsible for staying current, this creates a specific psychological pattern:

1. **Awareness** that something important is changing.
2. **Effort** to keep up — reading, experimenting, attending workshops.
3. **Recognition** that the gap between what you know and what exists is widening despite the effort.
4. **Dread** that falling behind carries professional consequences.
5. **Exhaustion** from the effort coupled with the sense of futility.

This is not a personal failure. It is a structural reality. The speed of development is greater than any individual's learning capacity. No amount of discipline, time management, or dedication will change this equation.

The productive response resembles the stages of grief: denial (I can keep up), anger (this is unsustainable), bargaining (if I just focus on the most important developments), depression (I am falling behind regardless), and acceptance (I will learn what I need for my work and let the rest pass).

Acceptance is not resignation. It is choosing depth over breadth. It is deciding that expertise in applying AI to *your specific domain* matters more than awareness of every new release. Faculty who deeply understand how to use LLMs for curriculum analysis do not need to also track every advance in computer vision.

### Type 2: The Strategic Thinking Trap

This form of burnout is less documented and more insidious. It affects people who have moved past the learning curve and are now using AI tools intensively and effectively.

The dynamic works like this:

The quality of LLM output scales directly with the quality of input. A well-considered prompt with carefully structured context produces dramatically better results than a casual query. This means that *thinking* — strategic, structured, careful thinking about what to ask and how to frame it — becomes extraordinarily high-leverage work.

This creates a feedback loop:

1. Better thinking produces better AI output.
2. Better AI output confirms the value of investing more thought.
3. The high return on thinking makes it addictive — each hour of strategic work produces visible, tangible, high-quality results.
4. Hours disappear. A session intended to be 30 minutes becomes 4 hours. A workday spent in continuous strategic-thinking-plus-AI-execution is more cognitively demanding than most people expect.
5. The exhaustion is real but difficult to name, because from the outside it looks like "just typing."

This is not the exhaustion of tedious work. It is the exhaustion of sustained high-intensity cognitive effort — the same kind that chess players, surgeons, and air traffic controllers experience. The difference is that those professions have institutional norms around rest and cognitive load. AI-augmented knowledge work does not, yet.

The risk is compounded by the fact that AI tools remove the *mechanical* friction that used to enforce natural breaks. When writing a report took hours of typing and formatting, the labor itself created rest periods. When the LLM handles execution and the human handles strategy, the human can sustain pure strategic thinking for hours without the natural pauses that manual work used to impose.

Professionals who use AI tools for 4-6+ hours per day in strategic-thinking mode report mental fatigue that is disproportionate to the apparent activity. This is not weakness. It is the predictable consequence of sustained cognitive effort without the friction-based rest patterns that previous work environments imposed by default.

### What to Do About It

There is no formula. But awareness helps. Recognizing that Type 1 burnout (the catching-up dread) is structural — not a personal failing — reduces the guilt. Recognizing that Type 2 burnout (the strategic-thinking exhaustion) is real — not laziness — legitimizes setting boundaries.

Both types share a common feature: they are invisible from the outside and difficult to explain to people who have not experienced them. The person experiencing Type 1 burnout looks like someone who "just needs to study more." The person experiencing Type 2 burnout looks like someone who "just sat at a computer all day."

For institutional leaders considering how to support AI adoption: the training question is not just "how do we use these tools." It includes "how do we use them sustainably."

## Project Showcase: Curricular Sequence Visualizer

This workshop's knowledge base — the catalog files, enrollment data, and job market analysis — was not assembled by hand. It was generated from a structured data project that demonstrates what becomes possible when institutional data is treated as a first-class engineering problem.

**The Curricular Sequence project** is a web application that visualizes all 146 degree programs offered across the Inter American University system. Students select a program and see every required course laid out across semesters, with prerequisites respected, credit limits enforced, and interactive drag-and-drop customization.

The underlying data:

- **146 degree YAML files** — every associate, bachelor, and certificate program in the 2020-21 catalog, structured as machine-readable data.
- **300+ course definitions** — titles, credits, and prerequisite chains.
- **General education templates** — the 48-credit bachelor and 24-credit associate requirements, with category-level override support for programs that substitute specific courses.

The application uses a topological sort algorithm (Kahn's algorithm) combined with credit-limit packing to automatically sequence courses into semesters. When a student drags a course to a different term, the system re-settles the remaining courses while respecting all prerequisite constraints.

**Why this matters for this workshop:**

The catalog files in this repository — `programs-business-1.md`, `programs-business-2.md`, `gen-ed-requirements.md`, and others — are generated from this same YAML data. The structured data that powers the visualizer also powers the LLM knowledge base. One data investment serves multiple purposes.

This is the pattern worth noticing: institutional data, structured once in a machine-readable format, becomes reusable across applications — student-facing tools, LLM context, accreditation reporting, program review. The bottleneck is not the technology. It is the initial decision to structure the data.

The project is built with vanilla HTML, CSS, and JavaScript — no frameworks, no build tools, no dependencies beyond a single vendored YAML parser. It runs on any web server. The entire data pipeline, from SmartCatalog scraping to YAML generation to web rendering, was built with AI-assisted development.

## Provocaciones — En Español

### La Pregunta de Matrícula

De 6,826 estudiantes en 2020 a 4,101 en 2024. Una reducción del 40% en cuatro años. La pregunta que nadie quiere responder: si la matrícula baja otro 20%, cuáles programas se cierran?

Un LLM no tiene política institucional. Cuando le das datos de matrícula, estructuras de programas y datos del mercado laboral, y le pides que identifique los programas más vulnerables — responde directamente. Esa franqueza no es sabiduría. Pero es un punto de partida que ningún comité de treinta personas producirá por sí solo.

### Lo Que la IA No Puede Hacer

- No puede tomar decisiones políticas.
- No puede reemplazar el juicio institucional.
- No puede sustituir la experiencia de dominio.
- No es una fuente neutral — refleja datos predominantemente en inglés y de universidades de investigación del mainland.

### El Paisaje Tecnológico

La lista de tecnologías de IA que un profesional "debería" conocer en 2026 incluye docenas de modelos fundacionales, herramientas de código, generadores de imagen/video/audio, frameworks de agentes, infraestructura de despliegue, y capacidades emergentes. La lista ya está desactualizada. Nadie puede aprender todo esto. Nadie debería intentarlo.

### Agotamiento por IA

**Tipo 1 — La trampa de ponerse al día:** La velocidad de desarrollo de la IA supera la capacidad de cualquier individuo para seguirla. Esto no es un fallo personal. Es una realidad estructural. La respuesta productiva es aceptación: elegir profundidad sobre amplitud.

**Tipo 2 — La trampa del pensamiento estratégico:** La calidad del output del LLM escala con la calidad del input. Esto hace que el pensamiento estratégico sea extraordinariamente valioso — y adictivo. Las horas desaparecen. El agotamiento es real pero difícil de nombrar, porque desde afuera parece "solo estar escribiendo en la computadora."

Para líderes institucionales: la pregunta de capacitación no es solo "cómo usamos estas herramientas." Incluye "cómo las usamos de manera sostenible."
